# Storyboard Writing

Use this reference for `write` mode. Turn catalog rows into shootable or generatable vertical scenes without changing upstream facts.

## Read before writing

Read `project.md`, `outline.md`, `characters.md`, `catalog.md`, the previous episode, and the next catalog row when available. For later batches, also read the latest review report. At a supplied-range boundary, a missing next row is a review blind spot rather than a blocker when the current ending question is already defined upstream. Resolve contradictions in favor of the most recently approved upstream file; report unresolved conflicts instead of guessing.

## Default 120-second rhythm

| Time | Dramatic function |
|---|---|
| 0–15s | Enter through an unstable image, active conflict, consequence, or precise question |
| 15–60s | Establish the episode objective, resistance, and information gap |
| 60–100s | Force a choice and deliver a payoff, reversal, or visual transformation |
| 100–120s | Show the result, then open one specific next question |

Adapt the proportions when the configured duration differs. Do not stretch a thin beat to fill time.

## Only storyboard format

Use one Markdown table per scene or time section:

| Shot / time | Framing / movement | Visual | Dialogue / narration | Sound / production |
|---|---|---|---|---|

Each shot should describe one stable visual state or one simple action transition. Include only details that affect composition, continuity, performance, or generation.

## Visual writing rules

- Replace abstract emotion with visible evidence: distance, gaze direction, posture, interrupted action, prop handling, blocking, weather, light, or environmental response.
- Repeat only the character identity anchors needed to prevent drift. Add scene state separately.
- Use concrete nouns, materials, colors, spatial relationships, and light direction.
- Keep dialogue speakable and subtitle-friendly. Let images carry information when they can.
- Avoid generated text inside the image. Put exact wording in subtitles, overlays, or production notes.
- Break complex combat into setup, decisive action, and aftermath rather than chained choreography.
- Avoid crowded shots unless the crowd is the point. Name foreground, middle ground, and background when spatial clarity matters.
- Do not depend on subtle finger positions or long hand close-ups unless essential.
- State vertical composition or safe subtitle space when it changes framing.

## Continuity locks

At the top of each episode, list only locks relevant to the batch:

- Character appearance and current costume stage.
- Current injuries, possessions, abilities, and known limits.
- Location and time carried from the previous episode.
- Knowledge each present character currently has.
- Setup that must be planted or paid off.

No new ability, resource, relationship status, or recovered injury may appear without an on-screen cause or upstream update.

## Production-aware escalation

Make importance readable through composition before adding cost. A close-up decision, a prop reveal, or changed blocking can carry a climax. Use large crowds, destruction, complex creatures, environment transformation, and multiple simultaneous effects only where the catalog marks high visual weight.

## Ending rule

Finish on a changed state, not an arbitrary interruption. The last shot must let the viewer ask one concrete question whose answer begins or materially affects the next episode. Record its hook family for the later review.

## Draft check

- The episode completes the catalog's advance and payoff.
- Shot times cover the configured duration without overlap or gaps.
- Each shot is visually stable enough to generate.
- Dialogue does not restate visible action.
- Character identity and mechanism limits match upstream files.
- The ending differs in function from the previous episode's hook.
- Romance and violence follow the project's content boundary.
